Learn the loop, one rep at a time

The field guide explains what the eight loop PM roles are. This page is how to build the skills. It covers the foundations under all eight, where to start from your background and a 12-week plan. Then it goes deep on each role: the ideas to understand, the core skills, three projects you can do without the job title, what to read, and a test for when you're ready.

01How these skills get builtThe method

You don't learn these roles by reading about them. You learn them by producing the artifacts each role produces, getting them judged, and doing it again on something harder. The artifact is the rep.

Every role below comes with three projects: a starter you can finish in a day, an intermediate one that takes about a week, and an advanced one that takes three or four. Each one produces something another person can check: a spec, an eval, a cost model, a threat model, a charter. None of them needs access to a lab.

Exhibit 1. The reps loop
Every rep ends in an artifact someone else can judge. Then the next rep gets harder: messier inputs, higher stakes, less help.

Aim for a T shape. Be literate in all eight roles, a practitioner in two, and an owner in one. Teams need people who can hold one slice of the loop and still talk to the other seven, because most failures happen where two slices meet.

02The foundations under all eightBuild these first

Seven skill areas run under every role. Each role leans on them differently (Exhibit 3), but all eight use all seven. Building them first makes every deep dive faster. Each comes with a first rep you can start this week.

F1Model fluency

Use the models every day on real work, and keep a failure log: date, model, task, what went wrong. You can't judge readiness, behavior or cost for a tool you don't use.

First repLog 20 failures over two weeks, then group them.

F2Evals and error analysis

Read 50 real outputs before you write any metric. Group the failures, count them, and write checks only for the biggest groups. This one habit sits under every role.

First repDo it on any AI feature you use and write up the top three failure groups.

F3Applied statistics

Confidence intervals, noise floors, base rates, the rule of three and paired comparisons. Five ideas cover most decisions these roles make (Exhibit 4).

First repTake one vendor's headline accuracy claim and put a confidence interval on it.

F4Building

Python, SQL and a notebook: enough to call an API, run an eval and plot the result. With coding agents this takes weeks, not a degree, and it frees you from waiting for an engineering slot.

First repScript a 30-case eval end to end, from inputs to a results table.

F5Unit economics

Cost per successful task, gross margin and capacity. A spreadsheet is enough. Most AI product arguments end once someone puts the cost per task on the table.

First repEstimate what one AI feature you use costs per task, and what it would need to charge.

F6Risk and policy

Threat models, permissions, and reading a rule for what it forces in the product. Every role that ships to users needs it, not only Safeguards.

First repList everything an AI agent you use is allowed to do, and mark the actions that can't be undone.

F7Customer and domain depth

Process discovery, segments and pilots with baselines. It's the classic PM skill, now priced in error costs.

First repShadow someone for an hour doing a job an agent might take over, and map the steps.

+Writing decisions down

Thresholds before results, decision records, and claims with a denominator and a date. It's what makes the other seven visible to the people who decide.

First repWrite one decision record for a choice you made this month, with the evidence that would reverse it.

Exhibit 4. How many cases you need
The most common mistake in AI product decisions is trusting a small eval. With 30 cases, a 90% pass rate could really be anywhere from about 79% to 100%.
Comparing two models: to trust a 3-point gap between pass rates measured on separate samples, you need roughly 770 cases each. Comparing both models on the same cases and counting where they disagree (a paired test) needs far fewer.

05Deep dive: Data & eval PMStage: Data

The skill underneath: turning "good" into something two strangers would label the same way, then proving the data actually moved the model.

What you need to understand

Inter-annotator agreement

How often labelers agree once chance is removed: Cohen's kappa for two raters, Krippendorff's alpha for more. Low agreement usually means an ambiguous spec, not careless people.

Gold set

Items labeled by your own experts and hidden from labelers and vendors. It scores label quality, so whoever it scores must never see it.

Rubric vs pairwise preference

Grading one output against a rubric, or picking the better of two. People often agree more easily on pairs; rubrics tell you why one is better.

Judge alignment

An LLM grader is usable only after you measure how often it agrees with humans on the same items, for passes and fails separately. Graders' own criteria also shift as they see more outputs.

Noise floor

How much a score moves across reruns with different seeds. A lift smaller than about twice that spread is not a result.

Target and guard slices

The slice the new data should improve, and the slices it must not hurt. Write both down before the run.

Contamination and saturation

Test items leaking into training data, and benchmarks everyone tops out on. Either one makes a score stop meaning anything.

Provenance and cost per accepted label

Where each item came from, under what license, with whose consent. And total spend, including review and rework, divided by the labels that pass QA.

The core skills

  1. Write guidelines that survive strangers. A qualified outsider with only your document matches your expert on the hard cases, not just the easy ones.
  2. Run the calibration loop. Label a batch, measure agreement, read every disagreement, fix the guidelines, repeat until agreement holds.
  3. Design a lift test. Hypothesis, target and guard slices, a control arm, the noise floor, and a scale, revise or kill rule written before the run.
  4. Design a benchmark that keeps meaning something. A private split, contamination checks, a refresh plan, and a path from any score down to the items behind it.

06Deep dive: Model PMStage: Model

The skill underneath: describing model behavior precisely enough to measure, train toward and gate.

What you need to understand

Pretraining and post-training

Pretraining builds broad capability from raw text. Post-training shapes behavior through supervised fine-tuning, preference tuning such as RLHF or DPO, and reinforcement learning on checkable tasks. Know which one can fix which gap.

The build ladder

Prompt, then better context or retrieval, then fine-tune, post-train, pretrain. Each rung costs more and is harder to undo, so climb only when the rung below has failed on evidence.

Behavior spec

A table of current versus ideal behavior, with the principle behind each row. OpenAI's Model Spec and Anthropic's constitution for Claude are public examples.

Hero use cases

The 10 to 30 tasks the model must be great at, each with an ideal answer. They anchor both the eval and the roadmap.

Slices and regressions

Cuts of the eval by language, customer, task or difficulty. Averages hide regressions; slices find them.

Win rate vs task success

Win rate says which output people prefer. Task success says whether the job got done. You usually need both.

Rule of three and over-refusal

Zero failures in n cases bounds the failure rate at about 3/n. And refusing safe requests is a quality failure, so track it next to harmful completions.

Versions and deprecation

Pin versions, publish change notes, give migration windows. Customers build on behavior, not on model names.

The core skills

  1. Write the model requirements doc. Hero use cases, ideal answers, and the quality tier each one needs (about 60%, 95% or 99.9%).
  2. Find where a gap lives. Model, context, harness or product, before anyone trains anything.
  3. Run a release scorecard. A frozen eval set, blockers agreed before candidates exist, and regressed rows read by hand.
  4. Run a migration. Change notes, staged rollout, rollback, and help for the customers who break.

07Deep dive: Research PMStage: Model

The skill underneath: judging what just became possible, sooner and more honestly than everyone else.

What you need to understand

Reading a paper for product

Find the claim, the setup, the baseline, what was released and what was never tested. Read in three passes: skim, then figures and method, then details only if it matters to you.

Benchmark vs your distribution

A benchmark win is a hypothesis about your data, not evidence about it. Measure on your own cases before you change a roadmap.

The almost-possible list

Capabilities your users need, each with today's measured state, the threshold that would flip it, and the trigger to watch.

Readiness levels

L0 claim, L1 demo, L2 prototype, L3 preview, L4 production. Score reliability, eval coverage, cost and safety; the weakest one sets the level.

Demo vs product

Hand-picked inputs, an expert driving, one run, hidden cost. Rerun on 20 to 50 messy inputs; a drop of over 20 points means it was a demo.

0-to-1 signals

Leaning in, asking for access, trying a second task unprompted, coming back within a week. Not DAU, which at eight users measures noise.

Kill criteria and pivot budgets

Written before the bet starts. Expect three to five pivots before a bet works or clearly doesn't.

Capability trends

By METR's measure, the length of software tasks models can finish has been doubling roughly every seven months. Trends like this tell you when to place a bet.

The core skills

  1. Brief any release within 48 hours, measured on your own cases, ending in one call: adopt, prototype, watch or ignore.
  2. Prototype it yourself with coding agents, in days rather than sprints.
  3. Read user sessions for behavior, not opinions or politeness.
  4. Run a portfolio of bets with seasons, kill criteria and public claims you can defend.

08Deep dive: Safeguards PMStage: Guardrails

The skill underneath: pricing mistakes in both directions, and assuming the attacker adapts.

What you need to understand

Base rates

When abuse is rare, most flags are false even for a good classifier. At 0.1% prevalence, 95% recall and a 1% false-positive rate, fewer than 1 in 11 flags are real.

Operating points

Pick the threshold from what each kind of error costs. F1 treats a missed abuser and a blocked customer as equal, and they almost never are.

Measuring prevalence

Estimate it from an independent random sample, never from your own flags, which only show what the classifier already catches.

Enforcement ladder

Warn, limit, verify, block, ban, with an appeal path. Match the action to the confidence and the harm.

Prompt injection

Instructions hidden in content the model reads. Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta" is private data, untrusted content and a way to send data out. An agent with all three can be made to leak.

Adaptive vs static testing

A fixed attack set measures yesterday's attacker. Real attackers retry and change tactics, so test the way they do.

Least privilege for agents

Anyone who can talk to the agent can trigger anything it's allowed to do. Cap the blast radius and confirm irreversible actions.

Threat model

Who attacks, what they gain, what one success costs you and the victim, and what stands in the way besides a classifier.

The core skills

  1. Threat-model a feature before launch: fraud, account farms, prompt injection, cost exploits, harmful content.
  2. Set and record threshold decisions, priced in dollars and harm on both sides.
  3. Red-team adaptively and measure attack success before and after each fix.
  4. Run incidents and turn each one into a standing metric.

09Deep dive: Inference PMStage: Serving

The skill underneath: connecting GPU-hours to the cost of a finished task and the latency a person actually feels.

What you need to understand

Prefill and decode

Reading the prompt is compute-heavy and sets time to first token. Generating is limited by memory bandwidth and sets tokens per second. They respond to different fixes.

Batching

Serving many requests at once raises throughput until latency breaks the SLO. Continuous batching keeps the GPU full as requests come and go.

KV cache

Memory that grows with context length times concurrent users. It, not compute, often caps how many users a GPU can serve. PagedAttention manages it like virtual memory.

Utilization

The share of paid GPU time doing useful work. Halve it and your cost per token doubles.

Quantization and distillation

Smaller numbers or smaller models: cheaper and faster, with quality risk that shows up in specific slices, often languages, before it shows in the average.

Caching, speculation, routing

Reuse repeated prompt prefixes, draft tokens with a small model, send easy requests to cheap models. Routing saves money only while escalations stay rare.

Percentiles and SLOs

p50 is the typical user; p95 and p99 are the ones who complain. Set budgets at percentiles, at a stated input length and concurrency.

Cost per task

Cost per 1M tokens is the platform's number. Cost per successful task, counting retries and failures, is the product's.

The core skills

  1. Build the cost model from GPU price, throughput at SLO and utilization, up to cost per successful task.
  2. Set latency budgets for each experience: chat, agents, voice and batch value speed very differently.
  3. Ship optimizations only after a slice-level quality check, and write down the trade.
  4. Price with margin and plan capacity with headroom and failover.

10Deep dive: Agent PMStage: Serving

The skill underneath: turning a messy human process into an agent whose results you can check, and earning autonomy one intent at a time.

What you need to understand

SOPs and intents

Map how people do the job today before you design the agent. Each intent, such as a refund or a reschedule, is its own small product.

Autonomy levels

Route, suggest, act, earned per intent on evidence. One critical breach sends an intent back down.

Verified resolution

The outcome checked in the system of record. Containment alone rises when the agent fails silently.

Tools and writes

What the agent can read, what it can change, and which changes can be undone. Enforce authority limits in the tool, where the prompt can't talk its way around them.

Escalation and handoff

When a human takes over, and what they receive so the customer doesn't have to repeat themselves.

Shadow mode and staged rollout

Run silently next to humans, then on a small share of traffic, with rollback ready. Treat each new language as its own rollout.

Simulated users and state checks

Test with a model playing the customer, and grade by the database state at the end, as τ-bench does. A polite transcript can hide a wrong booking.

Workflows vs agents

A fixed workflow is cheaper and more predictable. Use an agent loop only where the path truly varies.

The core skills

  1. Turn process discovery into a charter: the job, scope, KPIs and authority limits.
  2. Evaluate before launch on messy and adversarial scenarios, graded against the system of record.
  3. Run the weekly review that turns failures into regression tests and fixes.
  4. Build ROI and pricing the customer believes, because they helped build the model.

11Deep dive: Agent-as-user PMStage: Users

The skill underneath: watching an AI agent use your product the way you'd watch a user, then redesigning the surface for it.

What you need to understand

The agent journey

Discover, choose, integrate, get a credential, first call, use, recover, verify. Instrument each step the way you'd instrument a signup funnel.

Tool design

Few, well-named tools with clear schemas. Return what the agent needs, concisely, because every token of a response uses up its context.

Actionable errors

Say what was wrong and exactly what to send instead. Vague errors send agents into loops.

MCP, API or CLI

MCP when an assistant calls you at runtime. An API or SDK when a coding agent writes code against you. A CLI when the agent works in a terminal.

Docs for machines

Examples first, plain markdown versions of pages, and an llms.txt index that points to them.

The first credential

A keyless sandbox, a trial key a human claims later, a one-time browser approval that returns a scoped token, or a human pasting a key. Every key still belongs to a person or an org.

Agent telemetry

Tell agent traffic from human traffic, and measure task success for agents separately. Most teams can't yet.

Easy for agents, hard for bots

Every step you remove for agents is one you remove for abusers too. Design the path with Safeguards, not after.

The core skills

  1. Run agent usability tests with real coding agents, from a cold start, graded by your own acceptance check.
  2. Review tool and API design for an agent reader: names, schemas, responses, errors.
  3. Write docs for machines without making them worse for people.
  4. Design auth and limits that agents can get through and bots can't.

12Deep dive: Audience PMStage: Users

The skill underneath: knowing one segment's rules, stakes and language better than the general product team ever will.

What you need to understand

Four roles

The user, the buyer, the approver and the affected party. In schools and banks they're often four different people with four different goals.

Error-cost asymmetry

Segment-specific behavior is warranted when the worst failure costs roughly ten times more, when the law requires it, or when the segment eval shows harm.

Rules to controls

Read each regulation for what it forces in the product: a default, a log, a disclosure, a consent step or a block. Then have legal check your reading.

Configure first

Measure the best-configured general product on the segment eval before building anything segment-specific. The gap becomes the roadmap.

The behavior ladder

Defaults, prompt and policy, retrieval, classifiers, fine-tune, separate model. Use the cheapest change that closes the gap.

Segment evals

Written and graded with domain experts, in the segment's real language mix, including code-switched text. Translated English evals miss most of it.

Design-partner pilots

A measured baseline, a signed success criterion and a decision date 8 to 12 weeks out. Without them, pilots never end.

Protective defaults

When you can't tell whether a user is in a protected group, such as minors, and the error costs are lopsided, default to the protective experience.

The core skills

  1. Research a segment: the four roles, their jobs and their error costs, ranked.
  2. Map regulation and trust to specific product controls.
  3. Build domain evals with experts, in the segment's language.
  4. Run pilots that convert or stop on their decision date.

Hand it your artifact and ask for the gaps that role would flag, ranked by how much they matter.
