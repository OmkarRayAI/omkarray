01 —— The order layers fall Countable first, priced last

A layer commoditizes the moment its quality becomes countable. Transcription had word error rate, so it fell first. Evaluation is last in line — because “did the call work?” still has no agreed unit.

Exhibit 1 — Layers commoditize in the order they became measurable
Each rung of the voice stack got a number, and the number is what let three vendors undercut each other on it. Evaluation is the one rung still waiting for its metric — which is exactly why it still sets price by value, not cost.
The tell: you can't run a price war over a quantity nobody can compute. Give “good” a trusted number and the eval layer commoditizes too — the whole category is a race to supply that number first.
02 —— What a transcript can't see Text scores the words; voice fails elsewhere

Text evaluation scores a final answer. A call isn't one — it's a real-time duplex process where timing, audio and tool-state each fail on their own axis, none of it visible in the words. Transcribe-and-score passes agents that felt broken to the human on the line.

Exhibit 2 — Eight ways a call fails, by how much a transcript catches
Ranked by roughly how much of each failure mode text-only scoring captures. The transcript owns the top and almost none of the bottom. Everything in rust is a way a call breaks that words can't show. Hover any bar.
Read it as: a voice-eval product is, at heart, a claim to measure the rust — latency, barge-in, endpointing, tool-state. The transcript is the easy 10%.
03 —— The shape everyone converged on Simulate · observe · review

Independently, every credible entrant landed on the same three moves — which means the three moves no longer differentiate anyone. They're table stakes now. The differentiation moved to the one edge the loop doesn't cover.

Exhibit 3 — The three-layer lifecycle, and the seam
The loop every serious tool now claims. The three boxes have converged. The dashed return — turning a real production failure back into a durable test — is still assembled by hand, in tickets and spreadsheets.

04 —— Four shapes, one prize Cut by where the entrant came from

The companies chasing voice evaluation don't start from the same place, and the starting line tells you each one's edge and blind spot. Cut the market by origin — not by feature, since features are converging — and it falls cleanly into four.

06 —— Who grades the graders? An eval score is a model output too

Most of these tools score calls with a model. But an eval score is itself an uncertain model output — the judge can be wrong exactly the way the agent it grades can be. More metrics don't fix that; they multiply the number of confident numbers you're now trusting blind.

Exhibit 6 — The judge vs. the human
Illustrative. Each dot is a call: horizontal = automated judge score, vertical = human score. On the diagonal the judge is trustworthy. The shaded corner — judge says pass, human says fail — is false confidence, the most dangerous output an eval tool can produce. Hover a dot.
The product consequence: a release-blocking metric needs a measured agreement rate and the humility to abstain when unsure — not a bigger dashboard.
“More metrics” is a marketing axis. “Calibrated metrics” is a trust axis — and only the second one survives a compliance review.
07 —— Where the value actually is The evaluation waterline

Grading a call already costs more than making it — so the money in voice is drifting toward eval. But not evenly. Metric counts and call volumes are countable, copyable, and heading for a price war. What compounds is the stuff earned per customer over time.

Exhibit 7 — The grader costs more than the call
Per connected minute: what it costs to run the agent versus what the market charges to evaluate it. Carried from The Voice AI Stack. Testing is now the expensive part.

The bottom two rungs — bind a production failure to the test that proves it won't recur, versioned — are the category's obvious next primitive, and they're the emptiest cells on the map above.
The features that demo best — metric counts, call volumes, protocol lists — are the ones heading for a price war. The unglamorous middle, calibrated judgment and evidence lineage, is what compounds per customer and what almost no one has built.
08 —— Is the problem even real? What the benchmarks say

A fair objection: maybe agents are fine and this is anxiety being sold. The benchmarks say otherwise. Agents that ace a task in text lose a large share of that ability the moment it runs over audio — and more under realistic conditions.

Exhibit 9 — The capability cliff
Direction, not measured values — the shape is the finding. The same task, moved from text to clean audio to noisy real-world audio, sheds capability at each step. Hover a bar.
Directional read across recent voice benchmarks (τ-Voice, EVA-Bench, and — on the evaluators themselves — Testing the Testers). Exact figures vary by setup; the robust finding is that the text-to-voice gap is large and traces mostly to agent behavior under audio.
The problem is real, specific to the audio-and-timing layer transcripts ignore, and recursive: even the graders need grading. That's a category with a reason to exist — the open question was never demand, only which shape and which rung ends up owning it.
09 —— The four questions that decide it Unanswerable from public materials today
Open question

Does calibration actually gate releases?

Everyone can compute judge-vs-human agreement. Do buyers block a go-live on it — or is it a dashboard no one acts on? Decides whether §06 is a moat or a slide.

Open question

Can real audio be legally replayed?

“Replay production calls” assumes you may. Consent, PII and residency rules may quietly cap how much of that headline capability is usable in regulated verticals.

Open question

Which artifact is canonical?

Call, ticket, test case, metric result, or release? Whoever's object becomes the unit of record shapes the category — and it's genuinely unsettled.

Open question

Do bundled platform evals win by default?

If a runtime's built-in evals satisfy most buyers, the voice-native pure-plays get compressed into regulated, high-consequence work. The whole bull/bear axis.
