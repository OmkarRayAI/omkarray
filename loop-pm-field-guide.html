Eight PMs, one loop

At AI labs and model companies the PM job has split into eight roles. Each one owns a slice of the model loop: data, model, guardrails, serving and users, all measured by evals. This guide teaches the eight. For each one you get what it owns, the questions it always asks, its core moves, one illustration worth remembering and a drill to practise. A four-week path and a self-check close it out. Each role also ships as a Claude skill you can use on real work.

01The loop and the eight rolesStart here

A classic PM owned features. In an AI product the feature is downstream of a model. The model is shaped by data, bounded by guardrails, delivered by serving and judged by users. Each new PM role owns one of those slices.

On 19 October 2026 I analyzed 7,813 open roles at 87 AI companies. 234 were PM roles, and 70 of those had titles naming one of the new slices: model, research, data, safeguards, agent, inference and the like. Most of the rest were classic PM roles in growth, platform and product surfaces. The eight role names are mine. The companies post them under titles like Core Models, Post-Training, Safeguards, Agent Development and Token Factory. Click a role in the loop to jump to it.

Exhibit 1. The model loop and who owns each slice
Work flows clockwise. Data shapes the model, guardrails bound it, serving delivers it and users judge it. What users do becomes the next round of data. Evals measure every step.
Reading it: two roles sit at Model (one owns a shipped model, the other turns new research into its first product). Two sit at Serving (one owns tokens and latency, the other owns agents shipped to customers). Two sit at Users (one serves AI agents as users, the other a human segment such as teens, banks or a country).

02How to learn this in four weeksTwo roles a week

Reading about these roles teaches you the vocabulary. Doing their work is how you learn them. Pick one AI product you know well, such as a support bot, a voice agent or an API you use, and keep it for all four weeks. For each role, run one loop:

  1. Read the role below in about 15 minutes. Say back, in one sentence, what it owns that nobody else does.
  2. Do its drill on your product in about 45 minutes. Write the artifact, not notes about it.
  3. Open the answer key and mark what you missed. The misses are the lesson.
  4. Run the role's Claude skill on your draft and ask it to critique your work as that PM would. Fix the two biggest gaps.
  5. Once a week, run the loop review (section 11) on the same product with every role you have learned so far.
Exhibit 3. A four-week path
The order follows the loop: start with the measuring stick, end with the people.
Week 1

Data & eval, Model

Learn to define "good" and prove it. Everything else rests on this.

Week 2

Research, Safeguards

Judge whether a capability is ready, and how it will be abused once it is.

Week 3

Inference, Agent

Make it fast and affordable, then put it to work for a real customer.

Week 4

Agent-as-user, Audience

Design for machine users and for one human segment. Finish with a full loop review.

03Data & eval PMStage: Data

The dataset, the labeling program or the benchmark is the product. This PM makes the data the model learns from, and the yardstick it is judged by, into things people can trust.

Owns
  • Data specs that turn a model gap into labeled examples
  • Labeling and expert-data programs, with their quality system
  • Data vendors and expert networks
  • Benchmarks, leaderboards and eval platforms
  • Provenance, consent and licensing
Always asks
  1. Which eval should this data move, and what lift is worth the spend?
  2. Could a qualified outsider label this from the guidelines alone and match our expert?
  3. Who wrote the gold set, and is it hidden from everyone scored against it?
  4. What does an accepted label cost, including review and rework?
Core moves
  • Model gap → data spec
  • Stand up labeling and its quality system
  • Run vendors on quality first, then volume, then speed
  • Batch lift test before scaling a purchase
  • Design a benchmark that resists contamination and saturation
Metrics
agreementjudge-to-human agreementgold accuracycost per accepted labelturnaroundrework ratelift per batchsaturation
Traps
  • Scaling volume before labelers agree
  • Letting a vendor write the gold set
  • Lift tests with no control or noise floor

Who hires it: Anthropic (Human Data Platform), Mercor (APEX, a public leaderboard), Snorkel (Data & Quality), Scale AI.

Exhibit 4. The batch lift test
Before you buy 50,000 labels, prove a small batch moves the eval. A lift only counts if it beats run-to-run noise and no guard slice gets worse.
Legend: grey = baseline, blue = after the batch, gold band = the spread across 3+ baseline runs (here 2 points). Numbers are illustrative. Write the hypothesis, the target slice and the guard slices down before the run.
Drill · 45 minYour support bot mishandles refund requests written in Hinglish. Before the team buys 20,000 labels, write the data spec and a batch lift test.Open for what a strong answer covers
  • The task, the rubric and 10 worked examples, including code-switched and angry messages, plus edge cases such as partial refunds and missing booking IDs.
  • The labeler profile, and an agreement bar the spec must clear before any volume is bought.
  • A gold set written in-house and hidden from the vendor and the labelers.
  • The lift test: target and guard slices, a noise floor from 3 or more seeds, a control arm, and a scale, revise or kill rule.
  • Cost per accepted label, and data provenance and consent.
04Model PMStage: Model

Owns a model, not a feature. Every version has to be measurably better for the people who use it, and the PM proves that before it ships.

Owns
  • The model requirements doc: hero use cases and the ideal answer for each
  • The behavior spec: current versus ideal behavior
  • The data flywheel from product signals into training priorities
  • Release gates, launches, versioning and migrations
  • Choosing, swapping and routing vendor models
Always asks
  1. How right is it on each slice: about 60%, 95% or 99.9%? Each implies a different product.
  2. Is the gap in the model, the context, the harness or the product?
  3. What is the cheapest rung of the build ladder that closes it?
  4. What did it do, what should it have done, and which principle says so?
Core moves
  • Write the MRD and pick the build path: prompt, RAG, fine-tune, post-train, pretrain
  • Keep one behavior sheet that feeds both evals and training
  • Gate releases on a scorecard agreed before candidates exist
  • Turn every production incident into a standing metric
Metrics
win rateeval by sliceregressionstask successp50/p95 latencycost per taskover-refusalWER/MOS for speech
Traps
  • Shipping on the average while a top customer's slice regresses
  • Editing the eval set after seeing results
  • Fine-tuning before trying better context

Who hires it: OpenAI (Core Models), Thinking Machines (Post-Training), Sarvam (Models: speech recognition, text-to-speech and evals), Cerebras (AI Models), Glean (AI Quality), Cohere (Agent Harness & Modelling).

Exhibit 5. The quality tier sets the product design
Decide the tier slice by slice. A model that is 95% right on one slice and 60% on another is two different products.
Drill · 45 minYour vendor ships a new model version that is 20% cheaper and "better overall". Decide whether to switch, and write the plan.Open for what a strong answer covers
  • Freeze the eval set and the baseline before running the candidate.
  • Compare it slice by slice, including the top customers and languages, and read the regressed rows by hand.
  • Agree the blockers in advance: a regression on a hero or top-customer slice, rising over-refusal, latency or cost ceilings, API contract breaks.
  • Plan a staged rollout with rollback, and a migration note for anyone downstream.
  • Add a standing metric for whatever broke last time.
05Research PMStage: Model

Sits beside researchers and turns credible research into product, fast and honestly. The job has three loops: spot what just became possible, prove or kill a product around it, and send what users reveal back to research.

Owns
  • The frontier watch and an "almost possible" list
  • Capability briefs: what a new model or paper changes
  • Fast prototypes and the 0-to-1 signal
  • The bet portfolio, with kill criteria
  • Readiness, the handoff, and honest external claims
Always asks
  1. What can it do now that it couldn't last quarter, on whose inputs, measured how?
  2. Which user problem does that unlock, and who has it badly?
  3. Did it cross a threshold on our list, or is it a nicer demo?
  4. What is the cheapest prototype a real user can try this week?
Core moves
  • Keep an almost-possible list: a threshold, a trigger and a user problem per item
  • Prototype within days; read behavior in sessions, not DAU
  • Run bets in seasons; let the team call the kill
  • Score readiness from L0 claim to L4 production
  • Review every public claim against evidence
Metrics
capability-to-prototype timebets graduated / killed / parkedsession signalscycle timeeval coverage at handoffclaim corrections
Traps
  • Demo theater: golden-path inputs, one run, cost hidden
  • A roadmap led by benchmarks, not user problems
  • Zombie bets with no kill criteria

Who hires it: Anthropic (Product Management, Research; Research for Code; Claude Science). Google Labs runs the same playbook: an almost-possible list, seasons and teams of 2 to 3.

Exhibit 6. Readiness: the weakest dimension sets the level
Score reliability, eval coverage, cost and safety separately. One weak dimension is enough to sink a launch, so it decides the level.
Drill · 45 minA new open model claims a 1M-token context window. Write a one-page capability brief: does it change your roadmap?Open for what a strong answer covers
  • The claim restated, with its setup, and what was released: weights, code and eval data.
  • Cheap disqualifiers first: license, hosting, latency budget, price per task.
  • 30–50 of your own cases, 3 runs each, against your current pipeline, with the failures read.
  • Limits, the cost and latency envelope, and what it does not change.
  • One call: adopt, prototype, watch with a new trigger, or ignore. Plus a test of one week or less that would flip it.
06Safeguards PMStage: Guardrails

Keeps misuse rare, cheap and recoverable without taxing legitimate users. Owns the classifiers, enforcement, appeals, safe access and incident response.

Owns
  • Threat models for features and agents
  • Turning policy into a spec ML can build against
  • Classifier thresholds, priced in dollars and harm
  • Enforcement ladders, appeals and verification tiers
  • Red-teaming, incident response and agent permission reviews
Always asks
  1. Who will abuse this, what do they gain, and what does one success cost us and the victim?
  2. What is the worst irreversible outcome, and what stands in the way besides a classifier?
  3. At this threshold, how many legitimate users get hit per day?
  4. Was the defense tested against an attacker who adapted, or a static set?
Core moves
  • Threat-model before launch: fraud, account farms, prompt injection, cost exploits
  • Set thresholds by severity tier and write the decision down
  • Warn, limit, verify, block, ban, with appeals
  • Test adaptively, and turn every incident into a metric
  • Review agent permissions: anyone who can talk to the agent can trigger what it's allowed to do
Metrics
prevalenceprecision/recall at thresholdfalse-positive rateappeal overturnstime to mitigateabuse $friction
Traps
  • Trusting a "99% caught" claim from static tests
  • Choosing thresholds by F1, as if errors cost the same
  • Measuring prevalence from your own flags

Who hires it: Anthropic (six Safeguards and Trust PM roles), OpenAI (Integrity), Higgsfield (Trust & Safety), Mercor (Trust & Safety).

Exhibit 7. Base rates decide what you can automate
Out of 100,000 users with 0.1% abusers, a classifier with 95% recall and a 1% false-positive rate raises 1,094 flags. Fewer than 1 in 11 of them are abusers.
Drill · 45 minYou're launching free credits for an AI video tool. Write the threat model and the threshold decision.Open for what a strong answer covers
  • Threats: account farms, free-trial cycling, refund and chargeback abuse, prompt-injection cost exploits, reseller wrappers, harmful content.
  • Costs on both sides: dollars lost per missed abuser, and the cost and churn of each blocked real user.
  • Operating points compared, with step-up verification set against a hard block.
  • An enforcement ladder and an appeals path.
  • Metrics: prevalence measured independently of flags, appeal overturns, abuse dollars.
07Inference PMStage: Serving

Owns tokens, endpoints, latency, capacity and cost as the product. The job is to serve fast, reliable tokens at a healthy margin, and to prove that speed never silently cost quality.

Owns
  • Unit economics: cost per 1M tokens and per successful task
  • Latency budgets and SLOs
  • Capacity plans, reserved versus on-demand, failover
  • The served-model portfolio and pricing
  • Optimizations, each with a quality check; on-device and edge
Always asks
  1. At what utilization? A cost per token without one is a guess.
  2. Throughput at which latency? Peak speed with no SLO is a benchmark.
  3. Which percentile, at what input length and concurrency?
  4. Cost per what? Per token for the platform, per successful task for the app.
Core moves
  • Build the cost model from GPU price, throughput and utilization
  • Set latency budgets: time to first token, tokens/sec, p95
  • Plan capacity with headroom and failover
  • Ship quantization, caching and routing only after a slice-level quality check
  • Price above cost ÷ (1 − margin)
Metrics
TTFTtokens/secp95 latency$ per 1M tokens$ per taskutilizationgross marginSLA
Traps
  • Pricing from cost at 100% utilization
  • Quantizing because the average held while one language fell
  • Green dashboards over malformed outputs

Who hires it: Nebius (Token Factory), CoreWeave (AI Inference), Baseten (Inference Platform), Modal (Inference Engineering and Product Lead), Together AI (Model APIs), Sarvam (Model APIs; On-Device & Edge AI).

Exhibit 8. Utilization is the lever most teams ignore
The same tokens on the same GPUs cost twice as much at 30% utilization as at 60%.
Illustrative: a 4-GPU replica at $2.50 per GPU-hour serving 11,500 tokens per second within its latency SLO. Roll it up to cost per successful task, because failed attempts are paid for too.
Drill · 45 minYour voice agent costs $0.31 per resolved call and the target is $0.10. Find the path.Open for what a strong answer covers
  • A cost model per successful call, not per token, that includes failed and repeat calls.
  • Utilization and batching first, then caching. Routing easy intents to a smaller model saves money only while the escalation rate stays below 1 − cost_small ÷ cost_large.
  • A latency budget the voice turn must keep (about a second end to end), so savings don't add lag.
  • A quality gate per slice and language before each optimization ships.
  • Pricing and margin once the cost floor is known.
08Agent PMStage: Serving

Turns a customer's messy process into an agent that verifiably resolves work, then keeps it improving week after week. The unit of work is a deployment, not a feature.

Owns
  • The agent charter: the job, scope and KPIs
  • Autonomy per intent, earned on evidence
  • Flows, tools, knowledge and escalation design
  • Shadow mode, staged rollout and rollback
  • The weekly transcript review, ROI and per-resolution pricing
Always asks
  1. What does "resolved" look like in the customer's system of record?
  2. What can the agent read, what can it write, and which writes are reversible?
  3. What is the authority limit, and who signed it?
  4. When must a human take over, and what do they receive?
Core moves
  • Process discovery → SOP map → charter
  • Prove it on messy and adversarial scenarios before launch
  • Roll out in stages; treat each new language as its own rollout
  • Turn the weekly review's failures into regression tests and fixes
  • Co-build the ROI model with the customer
Metrics
verified resolutionescalation correctnesscontainment (never alone)CSAThandle time$ per resolutionclaim-error rate
Traps
  • Headlining containment: it rises when the agent fails silently
  • Enforcing refund caps in the prompt instead of the tool
  • Handoffs that make the customer repeat everything

Who hires it: Sierra (Agent Development, in six languages), Decagon (Agent PM), Scale AI (Forward Deployed PM), Harvey (Agents), Nous Research (Hermes Agent), Vercel (Software Factory).

Exhibit 9. Autonomy is earned per intent, never for the whole agent
Each intent (refund, reschedule, address change) climbs on its own evidence. One critical breach sends it back down.
Starting thresholds, not laws: tune them to the stakes of the intent. Enforce authority limits in the tool layer, where the prompt can't talk its way around them.
Drill · 45 minScope an agent for airline rebooking after cancellations. Write the charter.Open for what a strong answer covers
  • Intents ranked by volume × feasibility × safety, with a launch set of 3 to 8 intents covering 30 to 50% of volume.
  • Autonomy per intent: rebooking within the same fare can act; refunds above a cap only suggest.
  • Systems read and written, which writes are reversible, and the authority limits enforced in tools.
  • Escalation with full context, and what the agent says when a system is down.
  • Verified resolution checked against the booking system, shadow mode, then staged rollout.
09Agent-as-user PMStage: Users

Builds the product an AI agent can find, integrate, use and verify on its own. The user is a coding agent or an assistant, not a person: APIs, MCP servers, CLIs, docs and auth designed for machines.

Owns
  • The agent journey, from discovery to verification
  • Agent usability tests with real coding agents
  • Tool surfaces: API, MCP server, CLI
  • Docs written for machine readers
  • Auth, permissions, limits and pricing for agents
Always asks
  1. Asked to "add X to this app", does an agent pick us, and why?
  2. From an empty repo, how many steps until a verified first call?
  3. Which steps need a human, and is each one deliberate?
  4. How does the agent know it worked, beyond a 200 response?
Core moves
  • Map and instrument the agent journey
  • Run 3+ agents from a cold start, 5 runs each, graded by your own acceptance check
  • Small, well-named tools with errors an agent can act on
  • Solve the first-credential problem without weakening safety
Metrics
agent task successsteps to first callerror recoveryagent traffic sharedocs retrievalabandonment points
Traps
  • Docs only humans can read
  • One MCP tool per REST endpoint
  • A first key that needs a browser login and a CAPTCHA

Who hires it: Deepgram (Staff PM, Agentic Experiences, which asks for a former engineer). Adjacent: developer-platform and model API PMs at Together AI, Baseten and Sarvam.

Exhibit 10. The agent journey, and where agents stall
Test it like a usability study, except the participants are Claude Code, Codex and Cursor. Red marks the common stall points.
First-credential options, most to least agent-friendly: a keyless sandbox; a trial key the agent creates and a human claims later; a one-time browser approval that hands back a scoped token; a human pasting a key. Every key still belongs to a human or an org.
Drill · 45 minMake your product's API usable by coding agents. Design the test and the top three fixes.Open for what a strong answer covers
  • A cold-start test: an empty repo, 3 agents, 5 runs per task, success only when your acceptance check passes, plus one human developer as a baseline.
  • Results logged as steps, tokens, errors and the stall point for each run, with failures sorted into fixed classes.
  • Fixes: agent-readable docs (llms.txt, examples first), errors that say exactly what to change, and a programmatic first credential.
  • MCP versus API versus CLI, chosen by who calls at runtime.
  • Human confirmation kept for money, irreversible actions and permission changes.
10Audience PMStage: Users

Makes the model and the product work for one segment, under its rules and at its error costs: teens, schools, banks, clinicians, governments, a country or a language. It's the classic segment PM, now inside the model company.

Owns
  • The segment brief: user, buyer, approver, affected party
  • A map of the regulation and trust rules that apply
  • Segment behavior and a domain eval set
  • Design-partner pilots
  • Country and language launches
Always asks
  1. Who exactly: the user, the buyer, the gatekeeper and the protected party?
  2. What does a wrong answer cost here, and to whom?
  3. Which rules apply, and what does each force into the product?
  4. How much does the general product already do on the segment eval?
Core moves
  • Configure first, build second
  • Change model behavior only when the stakes justify it
  • Pilots with a signed baseline, a success criterion and a decision date
  • Country launches with evals written by native speakers, including code-switched text
Metrics
segment activationretentiondomain eval successoutput acceptancesegment incidentspilot conversiontime to value
Traps
  • Relabeling: "X for Banks" with no segment eval
  • English-only or translated-only evals for a local market
  • Pilot purgatory: no baseline, no decision date

Who hires it: OpenAI (Youth, Learning, Financial Services, Technology Vertical; it also listed Countries & Governments in March 2026), Anthropic (Public Sector, Claude Science).

Exhibit 11. Configure first, build second
The measured gap between the best-configured general product and the segment's bar becomes the roadmap.
Drill · 45 minLaunch your assistant for Indian banks. Write the segment brief and the regulation and trust map.Open for what a strong answer covers
  • Four roles named: the relationship manager who uses it, the bank that buys, risk and compliance who approve, the customer affected.
  • Rules that change controls: data residency and payment-data storage, data protection and consent, audit logging. Each one maps to a default, a log or a disclosure, and goes to legal for review.
  • A segment eval in English, Hindi and code-switched text, graded by domain experts, with error costs ranked.
  • Configure-first evidence before building anything bank-specific.
  • A design-partner pilot with a signed baseline, a success criterion and a decision date 8 to 12 weeks out.
11Use all eight: the loop reviewThe capstone

AI products fail at the seams. A model that is great on the eval but too slow to serve. An agent that resolves tickets but can be talked into refunds. A feature that works in English but not for the segment it was built for. The loop review walks every slice so none goes unowned.

  1. Frame it in three lines: what it is, who it's for, and what decision you need.
  2. Pick the lenses: score each one on stake and uncertainty, review the top 3 to 5 in depth, and give the rest one line.
  3. Run each chosen lens: what's solid, what's missing, the one change you'd make, the metric that would prove it.
  4. Surface the tensions between lenses. Recommend a side, and name what evidence would flip it.
  5. Close with a decision table: decision, owner lens, proof metric, by when.
  6. For launches, run a pre-mortem: assume it failed in six months, and trace each likely cause to a lens.
Exhibit 12. Pick lenses by stake and uncertainty
Example: a refund voice agent for an airline. Not every review needs all eight.
TensionLensesThe question to settle
Quality vs speed and costModel · InferenceDoes the bigger model, or more reasoning, earn its cost per task and its latency?
Autonomy vs abuseAgent · SafeguardsWhich actions are irreversible, and who confirms them?
One policy vs many segmentsModel · AudienceDoes one global behavior fit teens, banks and the public sector, or does one segment need its own?
Speed vs eval coverageResearch · Data & evalWas it tested on messy real inputs, or only on the demo?
Easy for agents vs easy for botsAgent-as-user · SafeguardsHow do you give agents a programmatic path without opening one to abuse?

12Check yourselfWhich lens leads?

For each situation, name the lens that should lead, then open it to check. Some situations involve two lenses. The answer names the lead and why.

01Containment is 70%, but customer satisfaction fell and repeat contacts rose.
Agent PM. Containment rises when the agent fails silently. Switch to verified resolution and escalation correctness, then read the transcripts.
02The 4-bit build is 30% cheaper, but Tamil and long-context quality dropped.
Inference PM, with the Model PM. Ship it only on the routes where no critical slice regresses. A slice loss is never averaged away.
03Coding agents keep failing to get an API key without a human.
Agent-as-user PM. Solve the first-credential problem: a sandbox, a claimable trial key or a one-time approval, while every key still belongs to a human.
04Labelers agree on only 40% of hard cases, and the vendor wants to scale to 50,000.
Data & eval PM. Fix the guidelines and recalibrate before buying volume. Low agreement is usually an ambiguous spec, not lazy labelers.
05A paper claims a 20% reasoning gain. Leadership asks whether to change the roadmap.
Research PM. Restate the claim, run 30–50 of your own cases over 3 runs, and make one call: adopt, prototype, watch or ignore.
06Free-trial sign-ups jumped tenfold overnight from a handful of IP ranges.
Safeguards PM. Account farms. Price the false-positive and miss costs, prefer step-up verification to blanket blocks, and track abuse dollars.
07Teachers say the tutor gives students the answers.
Audience PM. The segment's error cost differs. Build a segment eval with teachers, then change behavior using the cheapest change that works.
08The new model wins overall but loses on your largest customer's slice.
Model PM. A top-customer slice regression is a default release blocker. Read the regressed rows before deciding.
13The Claude skillsUse them on real work

Each one also covers what that PM owns and doesn't, its questions, metrics and traps, and dated notes from real postings and practitioners.
